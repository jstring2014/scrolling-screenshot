Designing for pages that never end

Scrolling used to be a way to reach the bottom. Now it is the interface itself — and most of our tools still assume a page fits on a screen.

Written by the design team · 12 min read · Updated this week

A layout that fits in a viewport is a layout you can reason about. You can point at it, hand it to someone, print it, put it in a slide. The moment content grows past the fold, all of those affordances quietly stop working, and we go looking for workarounds.

The fold moved, and then it disappeared

Every measurement we take of real sessions says the same thing: people scroll immediately, they scroll far, and they rarely come back up. The interesting content is almost never in the first screen. Yet the tooling around a page — sharing, reviewing, archiving — is still anchored to that first screen.

The consequence shows up in review workflows. Someone wants to flag a problem three screens down, so they take a screenshot of the problem and lose all the context above it. Someone else takes five screenshots and pastes them in sequence, and now the reviewer has to reassemble the page in their head.

“Send me the whole page” is a reasonable request that most tools answer with five separate images.

Containers changed the problem

Modern applications rarely scroll the document. The shell is fixed, and the content lives in a panel that scrolls independently — a message list, a data grid, a document body. The page height never changes, so any tool that measures the document sees one screen and stops there.

const shell = document.querySelector('.app-shell');
// the document is 1 screen tall; the panel inside it is 60
shell.scrollHeight === window.innerHeight; // true
panel.scrollHeight / panel.clientHeight;   // 60

This is why the same capture that works perfectly on a blog produces a single blank screen on a dashboard. The tool is not broken; it is measuring the wrong box.

Lazy content makes timing part of correctness

Images below the fold do not exist until you scroll to them. Fonts swap in a frame late. Virtualised rows appear and disappear as the scroll position moves. A capture that scrolls faster than the page renders will faithfully record a page that was never visible to anyone.

Rule of thumb. Never guess with a fixed delay. Wait for the specific thing you depend on — the images in this viewport, the font set, one painted frame — and put a ceiling on the wait so a slow third party cannot stall the whole run.

Sticky elements are not part of the content

A header that follows you down the page is helpful while reading and actively harmful in a stitched image, where it reappears every screen like a stutter. The fix is not to remove it — the first screen should keep it — but to hide it for everything after, and to be careful not to hide the container the content lives in.

What a good long capture actually needs

Measure the right box. Verify the scroll actually moved. Wait for the viewport to settle. Hide repeating furniture after the first screen. Stitch on exact pixel boundaries so no seam shows at fractional zoom. Split the output before the canvas runs out of memory rather than failing at the end.

None of these are exotic. They are the difference between a tool that works on your blog and a tool that works on the pages where people actually spend their day.

Where it still breaks

Recycled lists remain genuinely out of reach. If a row is destroyed the moment it leaves the viewport, no amount of scrolling will assemble the whole list, because the whole list never exists. That is a limitation worth stating plainly rather than papering over.

Everything else — the article, the dashboard, the report, the document, the grid — is a solved problem once you stop assuming the document is the thing that scrolls.
